Why autumn brings a rush of calls.

In deep cold, rodents rarely stay outdoors by choice. The first warm building on their route gets tested, which is why gaps matter more than neatness.

Nights are when rodents travel, so the first sign is often a sound after the television goes off. The season only changes how soon you notice it.

What triggers a call.

The first call from a Freeport home often starts with a sentence like one of these:

  • Insulation pulled out of a spot in the attic.
  • A sticky trap or snap trap from the hardware store that keeps filling up.
  • A dog or cat staring at one patch of wall.
  • A bitten wire or cable in the garage or on a car.

A line that never closes.

Calling in the evening is fine. Many people only notice the problem when the house is quiet, and the line is open then.

Not sure it is an emergency? Call anyway. A few minutes on the phone costs nothing, and the dispatcher helps decide how urgent it is.

A handful of quick steps.

  • Shut the noisiest room off and leave that door closed pending the technician's look.
  • Do not poke at droppings; stirring them lifts fine particles.
  • Avoid scattering loose bait, since a poisoned animal can end up dying inside a wall.
  • Jot down the room and hour of every noise or sighting.

A quick damp-cleaning guide

Treat these as plain summaries of federal pages rather than medical counsel. Anything about your own health is a question for your physician.

  • Rule one for droppings: nothing dry. A broom or a shop vacuum sends old waste airborne.
  • Open the windows and the doors, step out, and give the room thirty minutes to clear.
  • Skip bare hands entirely, because waste on a fingertip can end up in a mouth.
  • Drench the droppings using a disinfectant or diluted bleach, then leave the liquid to work for a few minutes.
  • When the bag is knotted and binned, wash your hands one more time.
  • Short of breath or running a fever after exposure? Call a doctor.
