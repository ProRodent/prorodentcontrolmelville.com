In Suffolk County's Town of Huntington lies the hamlet of Melville, which the census treats as a place. Several corporate headquarters sit along the NY 110 corridor, and the Long Island Expressway and Northern State Parkway cross the hamlet. For rodents, that mix means houses and commercial buildings both need a careful look.

Whether rodents live nearby is rarely the useful question. What matters is how easily your building admits them. Walking the foundation slowly at dusk with a flashlight will teach you more than wondering indoors.

Mouse, rat or something else?

Picture a pencil: a mouse can squeeze through a space about that wide, and a rat wants closer to half an inch. Norway rats stay at ground level, digging next to slabs, footings and stacked stone. Roof rats, if any are around, move along cables and limbs.

The trap, the metal gauge and the cleanup all depend on the species, which is why we name it before any number goes on paper. Your phone description is a first guess, and on-site evidence settles it.

How weather moves them.

A cold snap is the textbook trigger, though a thaw or a heavy rain can also send animals moving. Flooded ground drives burrowers uphill, and a warm house may be the driest shelter for a long way around. Once the first deep frost arrives, circle the lot looking for new holes, vent covers that have shifted and leaf piles against the siding.

  • Give every vent cover and pipe collar a shake before winter, and look at the two bottom corners of the garage door.
  • Put seed, kibble and lawn food in tubs with lids.
  • Keep firewood on a rack, clear of the wall.
  • Rake leaves away and trim brush so there is an arm's length of bare ground along the foundation.

While help is on the way.

A few simple moves protect people and clues alike. Do not sweep the droppings. Steer kids and animals toward another part of the home. Any trap you have already laid should stay where it is.

  • Snap photos of droppings and chewed areas with a coin beside them to show scale.
  • Note the time and room of every noise.
  • Close the door of the busiest room.
  • Avoid scattered poison and glue boards, because both create fresh trouble.

How a visit unfolds.

One mouse in a cupboard or a colony behind a basement wall, the job passes through the same four steps in the same order.

  1. Look.

    Work starts at the spot with the most evidence, be it attic, cellar, garage or yard, followed by a lap outdoors to find droppings and the openings in real use.

  2. Catch.

    Each trap is chosen for the species in question, set along the routes the evidence reveals and kept where children and pets cannot get to it.

  3. Close.

    Every hole, vent and chewed corner gets sheet metal, steel mesh or a sealant that hardens beyond what teeth can handle.

  4. Confirm.

    A later visit empties the traps, puts a hand on every repair and schedules whatever cleanup or restoration is left.

Telltale signs of rodents.

If more than one of these appears in the same corner, assume animals are living there and not just remnants of an old visit.

  • Droppings.

    Dark pellets roughly the size of a rice grain, found by baseboards, beneath sinks, on basement shelves or across attic batting.

  • Newest teeth marks.

    Fresh gnaw spots are pale and splintered on joists, pipes, cable jackets and cartons.

  • Night noises.

    A scratch, patter or dull thud in a wall or ceiling, usually heard once the house is quiet.

  • Nest material.

    Shredded paper, tugged-out insulation, cloth scraps or dried leaves bunched in a quiet corner.

  • Grease trails.

    Greasy dark marks on walls, pipes and joists trace a route that is used each night.

  • An odd odor.

    A stinging ammonia odor in a shut room, or a thick, sweetish stench pointing to a dead animal close by.

Two or three of these in the same corner usually mean live animals, so phone before they chew further.

Places callers name when they describe where they live.

M. Baylis House.

The M. Baylis House dates to about 1820 and is on the federal register of historic places. Callers sometimes mention it to say roughly where they are. It tells us nothing about your walls, so also give your street and the age and style of your own house, because an older building has more seams to inspect.

Arboretum Park and West Hills County Park.

Some callers name Arboretum Park or West Hills County Park to place themselves. The names help a dispatcher picture the area but say nothing about a particular lot. If your yard backs onto trees or open ground, mention it, since that changes where the outside lap of the house begins.

The NY 110 office corridor.

Corporate headquarters line stretches of NY 110, and many commercial buildings in the hamlet share dumpsters, loading areas and food-service space. If you manage one, say so on the call. A business site gets a different plan from a house, as the commercial page describes.

How your surroundings count.

A landmark gives a dispatcher a mental map and reveals nothing about your walls. A cul-de-sac, a garden bordering trees and a flat above a shop would each steer the visit differently.

With older houses and newer commercial space both found in Melville, an overlooked gap somewhere is plausible, though far from guaranteed. Carry a flashlight around the base of the walls, then phone about what turns up.

Buildings left empty by day.

Many people head out each morning, and a building left quiet for hours suits a mouse. If that describes yours, check pantry, cellar and storeroom corners on a weekend, and look at the attic hatch after trips away.

First questions from callers.

Is the line staffed overnight?

It is, on every night and holiday. If today has room you are offered a slot today, and otherwise the next opening.

How can I tell mice from rats?

Rice-sized droppings suggest mice, while bigger ones with blunt ends suggest rats. Loud thumping above you hints at something larger. The technician makes the final call on site.

Is it wise to put out poison while I wait?

Usually not at the start. An animal that eats poison can die within a wall, and clearing the odor may prove harder than the original trouble. For the legal limits, see the EPA page linked in the footer area.
