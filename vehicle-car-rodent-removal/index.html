Why a car appeals to a rodent.

Just after a drive, the space under a hood is warm, sheltered and full of soft parts to shred. A car untouched for weeks, whether on a driveway or at a storage yard, makes a quiet den with many ways up from below.

Rodents also chew wiring insulation, sometimes simply to wear down their teeth. The result can be anything from a nest in the air box to a dashboard light, a failed sensor or a car that will not start.

Where the technician looks.

  • Air intake and filter box, where nesting material often collects.
  • Spaces around the battery, belts and wiring harnesses.
  • Cabin air filter and the vents beneath the windshield cowl.
  • Under the vehicle: skid plates, frame rails and gaps beside the exhaust.

Droppings, shredded fabric and stored seeds point to a nest. Gnaw marks on hoses or insulation show active chewing. The technician photographs what is found so a mechanic can see it too.

Safety before starting the engine.

If you suspect a nest, think twice before turning the key. Nesting material near belts or exhaust parts can smoke or burn, and chewed wiring can short. Open the hood and look, using gloves, or leave it for the inspection.

Clean debris wet, following the CDC steps linked here. Sweeping a nest dry or blowing it out puts dust into the air and the cabin vents.

Making the parking spot less welcoming.

Move seed, trash and stacked materials away from where you park, and keep the garage tidy. If the vehicle sits in a garage that has seen rodents, the garage and basement page covers sealing the building. A clean vehicle in a rodent-proof space is the goal.

What to do in the interim.

  • Keep out of attics and crawl spaces, where shredded insulation puts dust in the air.
  • Do not disturb a nest, as a frightened parent may flee deeper into the framing.
  • Find the attic hatch and cellar door and keep both reachable.
  • Work out which outside wall the sounds seem to track.

What to do while you wait

A short pause before touching anything protects your lungs for next to nothing.

  • Trade the broom for a trigger sprayer.
  • Musty air trapped in a sealed shed is the thing to flush out first, so throw it open and stay clear.
  • Skin should not meet droppings, urine or nest material at any point.
  • Spray first, wipe afterward, and let about five minutes pass between the two.
  • Bag it: towels, droppings and any dead animal, picked up with tongs or a gloved hand.
  • Fever, muscle pain or hard breathing after a cleanup mean a doctor visit, and the doctor should hear what you handled. We are a pest company, not a clinic.

Car and camper queries.

How much harm can a mouse do to a car?

Plenty. Wire coatings, hoses and filters get chewed, and a nest can choke airflow. Lift the hood whenever a car has sat unused for a few weeks.

Is it safe to start a car with a nest in it?

Wait if you think there is one. A nest by belts or hot parts can ignite, and bitten wiring may cause faults. Get it checked first.

Will you fix chewed leads?

Our part is clearing nests and finding the way in. Replacing wire is for a garage mechanic or vehicle electrician, though we flag whatever damage we notice.

Does this cover boats and RVs?

Campers and trailers are included. Boats are handled the same way where we can reach them on land.

How do I discourage rodents from my parking spot?

Keep food out of the vehicle, clear nearby seed and clutter, and avoid parking against brush or stacked material.
