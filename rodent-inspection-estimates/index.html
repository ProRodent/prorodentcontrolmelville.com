The order of an inspection.

The survey runs in a set sequence so no area gets skipped. The technician begins at the roof edge and works downward: eaves, vents, chimney, siding, windows, foundation, doors. Then the inside gets its turn, from attic to garage.

Along the way, the technician looks for signs that distinguish the animal: droppings, grease marks, tooth marks, burrows, nests and smell. These clues matter more than a sighting, because one mouse in the open can mean a dozen hidden ones.

  • Roofline: ridge vents, soffits, fascia boards and flashing around chimneys and dormers.
  • Walls: pipe and cable entries, dryer vents, siding gaps and window frames.
  • Ground level: foundation cracks, basement windows, bulkhead doors and burrows.
  • Inside: attic insulation, cellar corners, garage door seals and stored boxes.

From findings to a written scope.

After the walk, the technician writes a scope. It states which animal was found, where it travels and how it gets in. Each recommended job appears separately, with materials and price, so you see what each step adds.

The reason is that rodent jobs run in phases. Trapping and sealing go first, with cleanup or repair coming afterward. Seeing the stages priced apart lets you spread the spending without losing the order that makes the work effective.

Older buildings and new ones.

Buildings in Melville span many eras, and the M. Baylis House, dating to about 1820 and a National Register listing, shows how far back they go. Age alone proves nothing about rodents, but older timber and masonry usually have more seams, so a slower look at the foundation and roofline is the practical response.

Getting ready.

A little preparation shortens the visit. Clear a path to the attic hatch, the basement door and the garage wall. Put pets in a room away from the work, and keep any chewed items or droppings for the technician to see rather than throwing them away.

If the problem is a business site, tell the dispatcher about deliveries, food storage and dumpsters. The commercial page explains how recurring visits and paper reports work.

What to do in the interim.

  • Park a car with debris under the hood and leave it alone until it is inspected.
  • Carry grass seed, birdseed and kibble indoors for the moment.
  • Bag any chewed object so the technician can handle it.
  • Locate the breaker panel in case a wire proves to be chewed.

Small steps before a visit

Treat the lines below as a short to-do list for the hour after droppings turn up.

  • Scrub lightly. Heavy elbow work throws dust and defeats the purpose of wetting.
  • Wedge a door, let the breeze run through and look at your watch.
  • Skin should not meet droppings, urine or nest material at any point.
  • Cover the waste and a margin around it with liquid, take a short break, then start wiping.
  • When the bag is knotted and binned, wash your hands one more time.
  • Not feeling well after contact with droppings is a question for a physician.

Questions about the inspection.

Do I pay before the quote?

The dispatcher explains the terms of the visit when you book. The written estimate follows the walk-through, and each line can be accepted or declined.

How long does it take?

Time depends on the size of the building and how much is hidden. A small house may take under an hour, while a large or complicated property takes longer.

Must an adult stay for the visit?

Someone should meet the technician at the door to describe what you noticed and open the attic, cellar and garage.

What if you find nothing?

Then the quote says so. A clean result is still useful, because it tells you what to watch and which openings to close before cold weather.

Can an inspection cover a business?

Yes. The technician walks the premises, including storerooms, loading areas and dumpsters, and notes anything that needs attention.
