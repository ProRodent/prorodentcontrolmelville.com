Feeders, Firewood and Compost: What You Leave Out Brings Them In

If it cannot wait for Monday, call anyway..

Most yards hold a few things that make rodents feel at home: a feeder with seed on the ground, a stack of firewood against the wall, a compost heap by the back door. None of them is a mistake in itself. Placement is what matters.

Rodents are practical. They look for food, water and cover, in that order of ease. A yard that supplies all three along a path to the house wall is easy to read. Fortunately it is also easy to change, and a few tweaks can make a visible difference.

Seed on the ground: the nightly buffet.

Bird feeders spill. Even a careful feeder drops hulls and seed, and those collect below. A dozen birds by day mean a pile by evening, and a rat or mouse will find it by dark. Cracked corn and sunflower seed are favorites.

  • Use a seed tray or a catcher under the feeder.
  • Sweep or rake the area every day or two.
  • Choose seed mixes with less filler that birds toss aside.
  • Bring feeders in at dusk if rodents are active.

Pet bowls, fallen fruit and unsecured trash do the same job. A dish left out overnight is a ready meal in every sense. Bringing food in at night is the cheapest control there is.

Firewood and lumber: cover with a ladder.

A woodpile stacked against siding hides runs from sight and lets rodents climb. Gaps between logs make cozy nests, and the stack sits close to the entry point they would like. Stack wood on a rack, off the ground, a few feet from the wall.

The same advice applies to stored lumber, pallets, old boards and tarps. If you cannot move them, lift them off the ground and check them regularly for droppings or nesting.

Compost: useful, if managed.

Composting is good for the garden, but an open heap invites rats. Heat and food scraps attract them, and the pile gives shelter. Use a closed bin with a tight lid and a base that blocks digging. Avoid meat, bones, dairy and cooked food.

Turn the compost regularly and keep it away from the house. If you see burrows near the bin, the animals have already settled and trapping may be needed. The rat page describes the process.

Lots next to open ground.

Melville has lots that border trees and park land, such as the places locals call Arboretum Park and West Hills County Park, as well as lots with only a narrow strip of grass. We cannot say what any one yard holds, so look at your own edges. A stone wall, a fence line or a hedge along the property is where animals travel, and it is where traps and checks should begin.

From yard to building.

Food and cover bring animals close, but gaps let them in. If your foundation has a loose vent, a cracked seam or a worn door sweep, the best yard work in the world will not help. Pair outdoor changes with the building checks in the sealing guide.

Where burrows sit beside the house, trapping comes before filling. The yard and burrow page lists the order of chores by effect.

A weekend plan.

  • Saturday morning: move seed, pet food and trash into lidded containers.
  • Saturday afternoon: move wood and boards off the wall and onto a rack.
  • Sunday morning: trim brush and ground cover back from the foundation.
  • Sunday afternoon: walk the lot and note any burrow mouths, then call if you find some.

Take photos of anything unusual. A technician can use them to plan the visit. If you notice new signs a week later, that is useful information too.

A caution on poison.

Putting bait in the yard feels like action, but it puts pets and wildlife at risk. The EPA rules on consumer bait exist for a reason, which our guide on store-bought bait explains. Reduce food and cover first, and let traps do the work.

A quick damp-cleaning guide

Nearly all the work is about avoiding dust in the air, and these steps show how.

  • Put the dustpan down. Sweeping crumbled droppings is how people end up breathing them.
  • Weeks of stillness behind a closed door call for a good cross-breeze.
  • Single-use gloves are acceptable, followed by a proper hand wash.
  • Spray until glossy, then walk away briefly.
  • Tie the bag tight and let it ride out with the usual household trash.
  • Whether a symptom is connected to rodents is for a medical professional to decide.

Still wondering?

Do I have to give up my bird feeder?

Not necessarily. Use a tray to catch seed, sweep daily, and consider taking the feeder in at night.

Is firewood a problem?

Wood stacked against a wall gives cover and a ladder to the roofline. Keep it on a rack, a few feet from the house.

What about a compost pile?

A closed bin set well away from the house is better than an open heap. Avoid adding cooked food or meat.

Will rodents leave if I remove the food?

Often they move on, though shelter and gaps still matter. Cut both food and cover.

How far from the house should things be?

A few feet at minimum for wood and brush, and farther for compost where space allows.
