The First Cold Night: Why Scratching Often Starts in Autumn

Something running above the ceiling this evening? Pick up the phone..

One evening the temperature drops, the furnace clicks on and something starts to scratch in the wall. The timing is not a coincidence. This guide explains what changes when nights turn cold and what a homeowner can check beforehand.

Most people remember the first time they heard it. A faint scrabble behind the pantry, a thump above the bedroom, a rustle in the wall near the water heater. Often it comes on the first properly cold night of the season, when the heat has just come on after months off. There is a reason.

What changes when the temperature falls.

Mice and rats do not hibernate. When night temperatures drop, food outdoors thins out and shelter becomes a bigger priority. A building is a dry, warm, wind-proof box with a pantry inside, and animals test its edges as soon as the cold starts to bite.

At the same time, the house changes. Heat comes on and warm air rises, escaping through small gaps in the roofline and carrying scent with it. That plume tells nearby animals where the heat is. The gap that was open all summer now has a signal attached.

Where they test the building first.

Animals check ground-level edges before roof edges, unless trees offer a shortcut. The common first stops are the same everywhere on Long Island.

  • Corners of the garage door and a seal worn flat.
  • Foundation vents whose screens are rusted through or gone.
  • Pipe and cable entries where the hole is bigger than the line.
  • Dryer and bath exhaust vents with loose or absent flaps.
  • Soffit edges and gaps where trim meets siding.

None of these is a sign of poor upkeep. They are simply the places where materials meet and weather works on them over the years. A quick lap with a flashlight will find most.

Early signs inside the house.

If animals are already in, the first evidence is usually small. Look in the pantry, under the sink, behind the fridge and along the bottom of the garage wall for droppings. Notice whether a food bag in the basement has a hole.

Sounds tell a story too. Light scratching just after dark often means mice in a wall. Heavy thumping on the attic side suggests larger animals. The article on ceiling noises explains how to read what you hear.

What hamlets like Melville have in common.

Melville, a Town of Huntington hamlet, mixes houses and office space with green areas such as Arboretum Park and West Hills County Park. We cannot say how those places affect any single lot. What we can say is that wherever a lot touches trees, fences or commercial dumpsters, the outside lap of a house should include that edge.

If you hear it tonight.

Stay calm and avoid poking the wall. Note the time and room, shut the door of the quietest part of the house if you can and write down what you hear. Keep food in sealed containers and take pet bowls up for the night.

In the morning, call with details. A dispatcher can offer a walk-through, and the technician will set traps before sealing the entries, so no animal is locked inside.

A realistic expectation.

No checklist guarantees a quiet winter. Cold pushes animals toward the nearest warm building, and some will find a gap that was invisible. The point of the routine is to cut the number of gaps and reduce the food on offer, so the odds go down and a problem, if it comes, is caught early.

If you have had trouble before, say so on the call. A previous nest or burrow in the same place often means the route is still in use, and the technician will want to see it.

Wet methods only

Treat the lines below as a short to-do list for the hour after droppings turn up.

  • Scrub lightly. Heavy elbow work throws dust and defeats the purpose of wetting.
  • Open the windows and the doors, step out, and give the room thirty minutes to clear.
  • Keep gloved hands away from eyes, nose and mouth until they are off and washed.
  • The soak keeps dust on the ground where it belongs, so do not skip it.
  • Double-bag when the contents are wet or have sharp edges.
  • If you feel strange later, mention the cleanup to your doctor.

More on this topic.

Is it too late once I hear scratching?

No. It means the animals are already inside, so trapping and sealing are the next steps. Acting quickly limits damage.

When should I check the outside of the house?

Aim for late summer or early autumn, ahead of the first deep frost. A second look after the first freeze catches new openings.

Do mice come in only in winter?

No. Cold increases the pressure, but a warm kitchen with food draws them all year.

Will turning up the heat make them leave?

No. Heat attracts them, and warm walls and attic floors are where they nest.

Can I fix gaps myself?

Small ones, yes, using hard materials, but only after you are sure no animal is trapped inside. A technician can confirm that first.
