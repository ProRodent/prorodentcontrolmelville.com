Footsteps, Scratches and Thumps: Reading Noises in the Ceiling

Noon or three in the morning, the phone gets the same attentive answer..

Something moving above your bed at 2 a.m. is impossible to ignore. The type of sound, the time and the spot each hold clues. Use this guide to put words to what you hear, so the technician knows where to begin.

Houses make noise. Pipes tick, ducts expand and the roof creaks as the sun leaves it. Animal noise is different because it moves, repeats and changes with the hour. After a few nights you can often tell what you are dealing with just from the sound.

A short vocabulary of overhead sounds.

What you hear.Likely cause.Notes for the call.
Light, quick scratching.Mice in a wall or ceiling.Note the room and time of night.
Pattering that races across a surface.Mice or small rats running along joists.Say whether it runs one way or back and forth.
Heavy thudding or a dragging sound.More likely a rat or bigger animal.Report how heavy it seems and how fast it moves.
Gnawing, a steady rasp.Rodent chewing wood or insulation.Mention where, since gnawing often marks an entry.
Squeaks and chirps.Young animals in a nest.Mention how long it has gone on.
Daytime rustling.Squirrels or birds.Tell the dispatcher it is daytime.

Treat this table as a first guide and not a ruling. The technician names the animal on site by droppings, gnaw marks and tracks, which tell a more reliable story than sound alone.

What the timing tells you.

Both animals move mostly after dark, with busy spells just after sunset and again before sunrise. A burst at dusk followed by long quiet spells fits mice. Constant movement through the night can mean a larger group. Writing down the times for a few nights helps the dispatcher decide how to schedule.

Weather matters too. Noises often rise on cold or rainy nights, when more animals seek shelter. If the sound seems tied to the furnace cycle, the animals may be nesting against warm ducts or a chimney chase.

Locating the sound without opening anything.

You can narrow things down safely. Stand quietly in the room with the sound and listen with the lights off. Does it come from above the ceiling, inside a wall or below the floor? Does it travel? Mark the spot with a sticky note if you can reach it.

  • Listen near the walls, not the middle of the room.
  • Note whether the sound moves toward a corner or a vent.
  • Check the corresponding spot outside for gaps in siding, soffits or vents.
  • Photograph any stain, hole or droppings you find along the way.

Offices and commercial buildings add ceiling plenums and mechanical rooms. If you manage one, read the guide on break rooms and mice to see where activity tends to start.

Why not to wait.

Animals in a ceiling do damage. They chew wire insulation, flatten insulation into nests and leave droppings that can carry disease. The longer they stay, the more material needs replacing. Early action usually costs less.

Do not block the hole or pour anything into the space. A trapped animal will gnaw a new exit, and you may seal a mother away from her young. Let a technician inspect and plan the order of work.

What to say when you call.

Dispatch will ask a few simple questions. Having the answers ready speeds things up and gets the right technician and tools to your door.

  • The street address and ZIP code.
  • The room under or beside the sound.
  • The hours you hear it and how long it has been going on.
  • Which children or animals share the house.
  • Any droppings, smells or chewed items you noticed.

After the visit, you will have a written scope. Trapping and sealing usually come first, and cleanup follows. If insulation has been damaged, see the cleanout page for what to expect.

Cleanup in the right order

A short pause before touching anything protects your lungs for next to nothing.

  • A dry rag is no safer than a broom, since it stirs the same powder.
  • Weeks of stillness behind a closed door call for a good cross-breeze.
  • Single-use gloves are acceptable, followed by a proper hand wash.
  • Soak, wait, then collect with paper towels.
  • Slide a carcass into a bag without letting skin touch it.
  • Fever, muscle pain or hard breathing after a cleanup mean a doctor visit, and the doctor should hear what you handled. We are a pest company, not a clinic.

Loose ends.

Could the noise be pipes or the house settling?

Sometimes. Pipes tick as they heat and cool, and framing creaks. Repeated, moving sounds that start after dark point to animals.

Is it safe to go up into the attic and look?

Better to wait for an inspection. Disturbed insulation can send up dust, and a startled animal may bolt. If you must look, use gloves, a mask and good light.

Does a sound at dawn mean something different?

Some animals are active at dusk and dawn, and squirrels are daytime. A technician can tell which species is involved from the pattern.

Should I bang on the ceiling?

It may quiet things briefly but does nothing to solve the problem. Write down the time and place of each sound instead.

How soon should I call?

Soon. Rodents chew wiring and nest in insulation, and a small problem grows quickly.
