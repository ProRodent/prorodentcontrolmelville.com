After a Wet Week: Burrows, Window Wells and Soggy Foundations

If it cannot wait for Monday, call anyway..

A wet week changes where animals feel safe. Burrows flood, ground cover turns to mud and the dry side of a building looks inviting. Here is what to check after heavy rain, and which small repairs help most.

Rain does more than soak the lawn. A string of wet days changes the map for every small animal in the yard. Burrows fill, runways turn to mud and the dry ground along a foundation becomes an attractive spot. Right after a storm is a good time for a short walk around the house.

What heavy rain does to rodents.

Rats that dig shallow burrows near a lawn edge or compost bin can be flooded out. They move to higher, drier places, and a building with a heated crawl space or a dry basement fits the bill. Mice, which live in smaller territories, may slip into a basement through any seam that opens when the ground shifts.

Water also moves soil. Gravel settles, mulch washes away and gaps that were hidden under a layer of dirt appear. A foundation vent that was fine in August may now sit above bare earth.

A post-storm walk.

  • Look along the foundation for fresh soil, new holes or collapsed edges.
  • Check window wells for leaves, standing water and cracked glass.
  • Inspect vents and screens to make sure none was knocked loose.
  • Look at downspouts, which should carry water away from the wall.
  • Peek into the garage and basement for damp patches and fresh droppings.

Take pictures of anything unusual. Keep a note of the date and the rainfall, because it helps explain when a problem began.

Window wells and bulkhead doors.

A window well is a tiny pit at the foundation, and it collects leaves like a bucket. Debris piles give cover, and a damp bottom draws insects. A cracked pane or a frame that no longer closes tightly gives access to the basement.

Clear the well, install a clear cover if it fits and repair broken glass. A bulkhead door should close flat against its frame, with no light showing. A worn seal can be replaced, and gaps beside the frame can be closed with metal.

Lots beside trees and open ground.

Many lots around Melville sit near trees and green areas such as Arboretum Park and West Hills County Park. We cannot say how weather affects any single yard, but leaf-heavy edges hold moisture longer, and that matters near the foundation. Rake leaves off the wall and away from the well.

Drainage as pest control.

It may seem odd, but water control is rodent control. Gutters that overflow, downspouts that dump at the footing and grade that slopes toward the house all soften the ground and open gaps. Fixing them also protects the foundation.

Keep mulch a few inches below siding and leave a strip of bare soil or gravel near the wall. That strip makes runs visible, and a technician can spot a new burrow quickly. Our yard and burrow page explains the order of chores.

If you find a burrow or a gap.

Do not stuff it with rags or pour chemicals in it. Photograph it and call. A technician can tell whether it is active and plan trapping before sealing the opening. For rats, the rat page describes the lap around the property.

Down in the basement, watch for droppings, damp patches and chewed items. When you spot signs, clean by the wet method and keep children and pets out of the area.

A realistic view.

Rain cannot be controlled, but its effects can be reduced. Keep gutters clear, keep soil sloped away, keep wells clean and keep gaps closed. These steps lower the odds that a stormy week becomes a rodent week.

Health agency advice in brief

Calling us is optional for this part. Health departments hand out the same instructions to anyone who finds droppings in a building.

  • Sweeping fast feels efficient and fills the room with drifting specks.
  • The first thirty minutes are for fresh air alone, with nobody working.
  • Leave gloves on until the job is done, then remove them inside out and wash.
  • Every dropping and stain should be dripping, not damp.
  • Collect first, carry second, and knot the bag before you walk anywhere.
  • Short of breath or running a fever after exposure? Call a doctor.

Loose ends.

Do burrows flood?

Shallow ones can. Flooded burrows push animals to move, and a dry building or a higher bank is a likely destination.

Are window wells a problem?

They can be. Leaves and debris in a well give cover, and a cracked window or an open frame offers a way in.

Should I fill a burrow right away?

Wait until you know it is empty. Filling an active burrow only sends the animal to dig another.

Does damp air matter inside?

Yes. A damp basement draws insects and softens building materials, so repair leaks and let the air move.

What if the foundation has a crack?

Note it and photograph it. Small cracks can be sealed with hard materials, while bigger ones need a mason.
