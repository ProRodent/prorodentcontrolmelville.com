Why a building appeals to mice.

Imagine a tiny renter touring flats. The wishlist is a dry nook, a pantry within a sprint and a concealed corridor connecting them. A stud bay behind the kitchen cupboards offers all three, and so does a pile of bins next to the garage door.

Around here the trigger is often the first hard frost. Mice that lived all summer under shrubs or inside sheds start testing foundations for warmth. Housekeeping does not stop this, and a cluttered closet is not the only cause.

Their active hours follow the dark, so the earliest clue often arrives when the television is switched off. Listen at the pantry wall, the furnace closet and any garage wall sharing a frame with the kitchen.

Reading the evidence.

We begin at the newest sign and work outward. Fresh pellets, a greasy rub on the baseboard and a cereal box with one nibbled corner draw a loose map, and that map nearly always finishes at a gap. Traps placed without the map catch a few animals while the opening stays as it was.

  • Cabinets under the sink, the slim gap beside the stove and fridge, and the closet holding the water heater.
  • The lower corners of the garage door, its rubber seal and the line where wall meets slab.
  • The eaves, the attic hatch, ridge vents and each place a pipe or wire pokes through.
  • The footing, where a vent grille, sill plate or crumbled mortar joint may have opened up.

First trap, then repair.

Snap traps or locked bait stations follow the trail and stay clear of small hands and paws. Mice run along walls and avoid new objects, so a trap flush against the baseboard works better than one left in open floor space.

Closing the gaps begins once the first catches show which routes are still active. Ordinary steel wool soon fails because persistent teeth pull it out. We use flashing, mesh and a hardening sealant, and the sealing page describes these materials.

Steps for the wait.

Leave the broom alone near dried droppings, because sweeping lifts dust. The CDC's damp-cleaning guide, linked at the bottom of the page, gives a safer order of work. Loose bait is better avoided altogether.

Storage of food counts for more than people expect. Move flour, cereal and kibble into tubs with lids, and take up the pet's water bowl at bedtime. This will not evict a mouse already indoors, but it removes the incentive that draws the next one.

A good outcome.

A finished job is a quiet one: no fresh pellets, and traps that stay empty. If a loft or wall cavity ended up badly soiled, insulation and nest cleanout may follow, and chewed cable or batting falls under duct and insulation repair.

Can't tell mice from something bigger? Request an inspection and quote. You come away knowing which animal it is and holding a written plan.

Sensible cautions.

  • Shut the noisiest room off and leave that door closed pending the technician's look.
  • Do not poke at droppings; stirring them lifts fine particles.
  • Avoid scattering loose bait, since a poisoned animal can end up dying inside a wall.
  • Jot down the room and hour of every noise or sighting.

What to do while you wait

A short pause before touching anything protects your lungs for next to nothing.

  • Rule one for droppings: nothing dry. A broom or a shop vacuum sends old waste airborne.
  • Musty air trapped in a sealed shed is the thing to flush out first, so throw it open and stay clear.
  • Skip bare hands entirely, because waste on a fingertip can end up in a mouth.
  • Spray first, wipe afterward, and let about five minutes pass between the two.
  • Double-bag when the contents are wet or have sharp edges.
  • If you feel strange later, mention the cleanup to your doctor.

What Melville callers ask about mice.

How small a gap lets a mouse through?

Rest a pencil on its side: a space that wide will pass one. Check next to plumbing, behind the clothes dryer's duct, around door frames and anywhere a cable enters the house.

Do mice go away in summer?

Not completely. Cooling weather is the famous trigger, yet a shaded wall or damp cellar can attract them in July. A gap left open in April may be the doorway in October.

Is it OK to lay out retail poison in the meantime?

Check with the technician first. A mouse that has eaten bait can crawl into a wall and die, and that odor is tougher to fix than the first complaint.

When will the noises stop?

That turns on the number of mice and the number of routes in use. After the technician has been through the building, you get a realistic estimate.

May I phone at 3 a.m.?

Yes. Dispatch is staffed all night, and when the schedule has space a same-day visit can be arranged.
