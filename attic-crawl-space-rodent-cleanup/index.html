Why cleanout is its own stage.

An attic or crawl space is where animals nest, store food and leave waste. Fiberglass or cellulose absorbs urine and holds droppings, and a compressed patch of insulation loses its insulating value. Taking out the affected material restores both hygiene and efficiency.

The order matters. If the animals are still active, fresh insulation is soiled within days. The job therefore starts only after trapping has cleared the space and sealing has closed the way in.

How the work is done safely.

Dust is the hazard, so the method is wet and slow. The area is ventilated first, workers wear protection, and surfaces are dampened before anything is moved. Material goes straight into heavy bags, which are sealed before they leave the space.

  • Ventilate the space for about thirty minutes before starting.
  • Mist the waste with cleaner and give it time to soak.
  • Remove soiled insulation by hand into heavy bags, never by dry vacuum.
  • Treat framing and floors, then let them dry before new material goes in.

Replacement and finishing.

Once the cavity is clean and dry, fresh insulation can go in to the depth the house needs. The technician checks that vents are clear, that ducts are intact and that no gap remains in the ceiling plane. A final look confirms that nothing has reopened.

Crawl spaces and low voids.

Crawl spaces follow the same rules with extra care. They are cramped, often damp and sometimes hold stored items. Tell the dispatcher how you reach the space, how high it is and whether pipes or ducts run through it, so the right crew and equipment arrive.

Describing a photo of the droppings or chewed spot over the phone gets the visit off to a quicker start.

Planning around other work.

Roof work, wiring and large drywall repairs belong to other trades. When the harm goes past insulation, the damage repair page lists which fixes we take on, and we say so plainly when a job needs a carpenter or an electrician.

Sensible cautions.

  • Park a car with debris under the hood and leave it alone until it is inspected.
  • Carry grass seed, birdseed and kibble indoors for the moment.
  • Bag any chewed object so the technician can handle it.
  • Locate the breaker panel in case a wire proves to be chewed.

What to do while you wait

Calling us is optional for this part. Health departments hand out the same instructions to anyone who finds droppings in a building.

  • Sweeping fast feels efficient and fills the room with drifting specks.
  • Closed rooms keep their air, so let a shut-up space exchange it before you walk in.
  • Keep gloved hands away from eyes, nose and mouth until they are off and washed.
  • Spray first, wipe afterward, and let about five minutes pass between the two.
  • When the bag is knotted and binned, wash your hands one more time.
  • Pay attention to your health over the following weeks and report flu-like symptoms to a doctor.

Cleanout questions.

Can I clean my attic myself?

You can, but follow the wet method, wear gloves and a suitable mask, and ventilate first. For large amounts of droppings, a professional crew has the equipment to bag and remove material safely.

Why not start with new batting?

Fresh material put down while animals are still in residence is fouled again. Trapping and gap closing have to come first.

Must all the batting go?

No. Lightly marked patches can be treated where they lie, but soaked or heavily fouled sections should be removed.

Is there a health risk?

Dust from dry droppings can carry hazards. That is why damp methods, gloves and ventilation are the standard, and why sweeping is discouraged.

What about odors?

Once waste and nesting are removed and the surfaces are treated, most odors fade. A lingering smell may point to a hidden carcass, which the odor page covers.
