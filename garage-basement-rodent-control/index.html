Why these rooms attract animals.

Garages and basements share three traits that rodents like: they are quiet, they stay cool and they hold stored goods. A few boxes against a wall create a dark corridor, and a loose weather seal supplies the entrance.

Add pet food, bird seed or a shelf of dry goods, and the room turns into a pantry. Rodents may not live in the garage, but they can cross it every night on the way to somewhere warmer.

How the walk-through goes in these rooms.

The technician starts outside, at the bottom of the overhead door, and looks along both corners and the threshold. Light showing through is a sign of a usable gap. Next come vents, pipe collars, the slab edge and any shed or lean-to attached to the building.

  • The overhead door: bottom seal, side seals and the track corners.
  • Walls and framing: gaps where cables, pipes or conduit enter.
  • Storage: cardboard, fabric, tarps and paper piles along the walls.
  • Basement: sill plates, window wells, bulkhead doors and floor drains.

What the evidence says.

Droppings along a wall tell you the animals walk that line. Shredded paper behind a stack of boxes points to a nest. Chewed bags of seed show where food was found. The technician records each sign and marks where traps should go.

Small habits with big payoffs.

Shelving off the floor and tubs with lids do more than any gadget. Boxes pulled a few inches from the wall let a technician see behind them, and rodents like that less. A weather seal on the overhead door keeps out more animals than most people expect.

In larger commercial spaces such as storage rooms and loading areas, the plan expands to include dumpsters and delivery doors. The commercial page describes those programs.

Older cellars take a closer look.

Block or fieldstone cellar walls have more joints than poured concrete, so the sealing list may be longer. Melville's older structures, like the M. Baylis House from about 1820, remind us how long buildings here can stand, and an old foundation deserves a slow look for gaps and rodent entry.

Next steps.

After trapping, close the gaps using the methods on the sealing page. If stored items are contaminated, the cleanout page explains the handling steps.

Once the visit is on the calendar.

  • Open up walking room by the garage wall, beneath the sink and at the attic hatch.
  • Do not shake out shredded paper or cardboard, because the dust drifts.
  • Keep any snap trap from the hardware store away from small children and big dogs.
  • Name the smelliest room so the search can start there.

What to do while you wait

Treat the lines below as a short to-do list for the hour after droppings turn up.

  • Mist stiff droppings before anything gets moved.
  • Wedge a door, let the breeze run through and look at your watch.
  • Skin should not meet droppings, urine or nest material at any point.
  • Cover the waste and a margin around it with liquid, take a short break, then start wiping.
  • Tie the bag tight and let it ride out with the usual household trash.
  • Diagnosing illness is not our trade, so anyone unwell after contact should consult a clinician.

Questions on garages and cellars.

What draws mice to a garage?

Shelter, cardboard and old fabric for nests, plus a sack of pet food or seed. A tired door seal finishes the invitation.

Are gnawed cartons worth keeping?

Soaked or heavily fouled ones should be bagged using damp methods and gloves. Set one chewed example aside so the technician can study the bite pattern.

Can I fix the door seal myself?

A new bottom seal is a common do-it-yourself fix, but do it after trapping so no animal is locked inside. The technician can tell you if the corners need metal.

What about a damp cellar?

Moisture draws insects and makes the space more appealing. Fixing leaks and running a dehumidifier help, though gaps still need sealing.

Is a separate garage or shed included?

It is. Outbuildings get the same survey as the house, because they are often where animals first settle.
