Why traps go where they go.

Rodents are creatures of habit. They take the same path each night, usually along a wall or the underside of a joist, and they dislike open floor. A trap placed where the animal already travels catches it. A trap in the middle of a room mostly collects dust.

The technician reads the route from the evidence: a dark grease mark along a baseboard, a line of droppings under a pipe, a worn spot in the insulation. Those marks decide trap type, number and exact position, and each placement is logged.

Snap traps and enclosed stations.

Both types have a place. Snap traps work well indoors along walls, in attics and in cellars. Enclosed stations keep the mechanism inside a protective housing, which suits locations where pets, children or wildlife might reach.

  • Snap traps: quick, easy to check and ideal along attic runs and basement walls.
  • Enclosed stations: tamper-resistant boxes for garages, sheds and outdoor runs.
  • Larger traps for rats, since a mouse trap is too weak for them.
  • No sticky boards, which hold birds and chipmunks as readily as mice.

Timing the return visits.

Trapping is rarely finished in a single visit. The technician returns on the agreed dates to empty traps, move them where activity shifted and note whether new droppings appear. When the traps stay empty for the agreed period, the animals are probably gone.

Sealing is the next step. If holes are shut too soon, animals stay inside the walls, where they either die or chew a fresh way out. The sealing page describes the next step.

Outdoor lines near open ground.

Properties near wooded park land, such as the places locals call West Hills County Park or Arboretum Park, can see different animal traffic than a block of attached buildings. We cannot say that of any one lot, so tell the dispatcher about brush, stone walls and fence lines, since trap lines often begin at those spots.

After the last catch.

When trapping ends, the technician can seal the openings, and after that comes cleanup if droppings and nesting remain. The cleanout page explains the steps. Meanwhile, keep food in lidded containers so no new animal finds an easy meal.

What to do in the interim.

  • Stow the broom and shop vacuum away from rooms holding droppings.
  • Keep the bleach capped until the full extent is known.
  • Set a coin next to each bite mark so photos show scale.
  • Estimate how long the telltale signs have been turning up.

Health agency advice in brief

Calling us is optional for this part. Health departments hand out the same instructions to anyone who finds droppings in a building.

  • Scrub lightly. Heavy elbow work throws dust and defeats the purpose of wetting.
  • Musty air trapped in a sealed shed is the thing to flush out first, so throw it open and stay clear.
  • Skip bare hands entirely, because waste on a fingertip can end up in a mouth.
  • Drench the droppings using a disinfectant or diluted bleach, then leave the liquid to work for a few minutes.
  • Double-bag when the contents are wet or have sharp edges.
  • Short of breath or running a fever after exposure? Call a doctor.

Trapping questions, answered.

Do you use poison?

We start with traps and sealing. Bait may leave animals dead inside walls, and the resulting odor can outlast the original complaint. Should bait be chosen, we name the product and the spot.

Are the traps safe around pets?

Locked stations and carefully sited snap traps keep paws and noses away. Name every pet and child in the home so placement can allow for them.

How long does trapping take?

One or two weeks is common for a small problem, and longer when many animals or several routes are involved. The return dates give a realistic picture.

Why not just seal the holes?

Sealing while animals are inside can lock them in, and they will gnaw a new exit. Trapping first, then sealing, avoids that.

What happens to the animals?

Animals we catch are taken away in a sealed bag, using the hygiene steps the CDC lays out for rodent waste.
