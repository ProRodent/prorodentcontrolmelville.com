Why sealing decides the outcome.

Trapping removes animals that are inside. Sealing decides whether new ones can arrive. Without it, a trap line can run for weeks and the problem returns the first cold night, because the same opening still serves as a door.

A thorough job begins with a count. The technician walks the roof, siding, foundation and garage and writes down every gap, its approximate size and the material that suits it. That list becomes both the plan and the checklist.

Where gaps hide.

The obvious holes are rarely the only ones. Rodents also use the corner of a garage door, the joint between siding and trim, the gap around a gas line and the screen on a foundation vent. A scan of all of those is part of every job.

  • Roof edge: soffits, fascia boards, ridge vents and the flashing around chimneys.
  • Walls: dryer and bath exhausts, hose bibs, cable entries and siding joints.
  • Foundation: vent screens, cracked mortar, sill plates and bulkhead doors.
  • Garage: bottom seals, side corners and the joint between slab and wall.

Materials that work.

The rule is simple: if teeth can move it, it does not belong in an exclusion job. Heavy-gauge woven steel mesh goes into larger gaps, metal flashing covers edges, and a hard-curing sealant locks everything in place. Copper or galvanized hardware resists rust.

Older buildings take extra patience.

Melville's buildings come from many periods. The M. Baylis House, which went up around 1820 and is a National Register listing, proves a timber structure can last. Older houses tend to have more joints where trim meets masonry, so inspecting them takes longer, and the sealing list is usually longer as well.

Whatever the age of the house, the method stays the same: find the opening, measure it and close it with hard material. A technician will tell you honestly if a gap is beyond a simple fix and needs a carpenter or mason.

After the last gap is shut.

A recheck visit tests each repair and looks for new gnawing. If droppings or nesting remain, the cleanout page follows. Seeds and kibble should stay in lidded tubs, and the inspection page covers the survey that should come first.

Before the truck is in your driveway.

  • Shut the noisiest room off and leave that door closed pending the technician's look.
  • Do not poke at droppings; stirring them lifts fine particles.
  • Avoid scattering loose bait, since a poisoned animal can end up dying inside a wall.
  • Jot down the room and hour of every noise or sighting.

Droppings, minus the broom

Nearly all the work is about avoiding dust in the air, and these steps show how.

  • Rule one for droppings: nothing dry. A broom or a shop vacuum sends old waste airborne.
  • The CDC puts the airing time at about thirty minutes before any work starts in a closed space.
  • Covered hands while you work, clean hands when you finish.
  • Soak, wait, then collect with paper towels.
  • Tie the bag tight and let it ride out with the usual household trash.
  • If you feel strange later, mention the cleanup to your doctor.

Questions about sealing.

Are steel wool and spray foam enough?

Not for long. Rodents tug wool loose over time and chew foam readily, whereas mesh, flashing and a compound that sets hard stay put.

How big a gap needs closing?

Assume a pencil-width opening can admit a mouse and about half an inch can admit a rat. Anything near those sizes should be shut.

When does sealing happen?

After trapping has cleared the animals inside. Sealing earlier can lock one in the wall, where it dies or gnaws a new exit.

Will this stop rodents forever?

No reputable firm can promise that. Sealing closes the openings we find, and a return visit picks up any that appear later.

Do you seal vehicles too?

Cars are handled separately on the vehicle page, because engine bays and cabin vents need different steps.
